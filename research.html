Does investing $25 a week really add up?

A simple look at consistent investing, compound growth, and the fees that can quietly eat into returns.

An educational scenario analysis · Approximately 4-minute read

Why I looked into this

When you're just getting started with investing, $25 a week may not sound like much. I wanted to see what those small contributions might add up to over time—and how much of a difference investment fees could make.

This builds on an earlier, more detailed comparison I worked on about long-term investment fees. Here I've kept the example simpler so the numbers are easier to follow.

The question

If someone invests $25 each week, do small differences in annual fund fees really matter over 10, 20, or 30 years?

My expectation: Both portfolios should grow under the model's assumed positive return, but the lower-fee portfolio should end up further ahead as time passes.

My approach

I kept the hypothetical investments identical except for one detail: the ongoing annual fee. I assumed $25 a week (converted to $108.33 per month), no starting balance, a constant 6% gross annual return, and fees of either 0.10% or 1.00% each year.

For simplicity, I subtracted the fee from the assumed annual return, divided by 12, and compounded monthly. Contributions happen at the end of each month. This is a classroom-style approximation, not how every fund calculates or deducts fees in practice.

For context, the Investment Company Institute reported an average expense ratio of 0.14% for index equity ETFs in 2025. The 0.10% figure is a low-fee illustration, while 1.00% is deliberately a higher-fee comparison—not an estimate of what all funds charge.

What I took away

  1. Consistency matters. Small contributions can add up over longer periods, assuming returns are positive over time.
  2. Costs matter more as a portfolio grows. Money taken out in fees cannot continue compounding.
  3. Lower fees aren't the whole decision. Risk, diversification, taxes, liquidity, service and investment quality also matter. A more expensive product may offer benefits, but those benefits should justify the cost.

My main takeaway is simple: understanding a fee is part of understanding an investment. The SEC has similarly explained that even small ongoing charges can make a meaningful difference to a portfolio's value over time.

What this model doesn't prove

It doesn't predict what an actual stock or fund will earn. Markets fluctuate, and returns can be negative. Fees may be charged at different times or in different ways, and inflation reduces purchasing power. I also didn't account for taxes, account charges, contribution changes or transaction costs. The 0.10% and 1.00% rates are examples, not recommendations or claims about what a specific product charges.

A useful follow-up would be to compare actual fund expense ratios and risk exposures using their prospectuses rather than relying on an assumed return.

Sources & methodology

  1. SEC Investor Bulletin (2025): How Fees and Expenses Affect Your Investment Portfolio — background on why fees reduce compounded investment values.
  2. SEC Investor Bulletin (2025): Mutual Fund and ETF Fees and Expenses — definitions and disclosure of fund costs.
  3. Investment Company Institute (March 2026): Trends in the Expenses and Fees of Funds, 2025 — context on reported industry expense ratios.

Reproducible formula: for monthly contribution P, monthly assumed net rate r and n months, future value = P × ((1 + r)n − 1) / r. Here, P = $25 × 52 / 12, r = (0.06 − annual fee)/12, n = 12 × years.
